Alcohol-Linked Cancer Deaths Have Doubled Since 1990, New Study Finds

By Headline Wire Today Staff Published April 6, 2026 6 min read

Deaths from cancers linked to alcohol consumption have more than doubled in the United States over the past three decades, according to a new study published August 2 in The Lancet Regional Health – Americas. Researchers found alcohol-attributable cancer deaths rose from about 11,400 in 1990 to more than 23,100 in 2023.

The study, led by researchers at Sylvester Comprehensive Cancer Center at the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine, used three decades of Global Burden of Disease data to track the trend. The findings challenge the common assumption that alcohol-related cancer risk is limited to liver disease.

It's not just the liver

Researchers found rising incidence not only in liver cancer but also in colorectal, breast, esophageal, pancreatic and other cancers. "The most surprising finding was the breadth of alcohol's potential impact across cancer types," said Dr. Chinmay Jani, the study's corresponding author and chief fellow in hematology and oncology at Sylvester.

"Although its relationship with liver cancer is widely recognized, its contribution to cancers such as colorectal, esophageal, breast, pancreatic and prostate cancer is less well understood by the public," Jani said in a news release.

The study also found that it takes very little alcohol to raise cancer risk. Prior research cited by the authors shows elevated risk starting at intake levels as low as 5 to 15 grams of alcohol per day. The CDC defines a standard U.S. drink as containing 14 grams of pure alcohol, meaning even less than one drink a day may carry measurable risk.

The study has limitations the authors acknowledge, including its observational design and reliance on a database framework that can produce incomplete data in some regions and years.

Dr. Gilberto Lopes, the study's senior author and chief of the Division of Medical Oncology at Sylvester, said the findings reinforce how much lifestyle factors influence cancer risk. "What this study makes clear is that alcohol-related cancer risk is not limited to one disease or one group of people," Lopes said.

"As physicians and cancer researchers, we have an opportunity to help patients understand that alcohol is a modifiable risk factor and that even small, informed changes can be part of a broader strategy to reduce cancer risk in our community," he said.

The researchers say the study underscores the need for broader public awareness of alcohol's cancer risks beyond the liver, and for continued monitoring as consumption patterns shift.
